🩺 Memory tests doctors use

When someone is worried about memory, a clinician combines a conversation about everyday life with short paper-and-pencil tests and, sometimes, longer specialist testing. Here's what the common tests involve, so you know what to expect.

ℹ️ This page explains the tests; it does not include them. These tests are meant to be given and scored by trained professionals, and several are copyrighted or licensed (see official links and permissions). No single score diagnoses MCI or dementia.[1]

Side-by-side comparison

Times and score ranges below are from the comparison tables in the Alzheimer's Association clinical practice guideline (2024)[1] unless another source is shown.

TestWhat it measuresWho gives itTypical timeKey limitations
MoCA
Montreal Cognitive Assessment		 Several thinking areas: orientation, memory (delayed recall of words), language, attention, visuospatial skills and executive function. Scored out of 30.[1] A clinician or health professional. Official training and certification has been required to give and score it since 2019.[3] 10–15 minutes[1] Scores are strongly affected by low education (an adapted version exists for people with less than 4 years of schooling).[1] A screening test: it flags possible problems but does not diagnose. More sensitive to MCI than the MMSE in its original study (90% vs 18%).[2]
Mini-Cog Short-term memory (recalling three words) plus a clock-drawing task for visuospatial and executive skills. Scored out of 5.[1] A clinician; it needs minimal training.[4] 2–4 minutes[1] Validated as a screening test, not a diagnostic test; better at picking up dementia than MCI, and may miss MCI in people with high intellectual ability.[1]
SLUMS
Saint Louis University Mental Status exam		 11 questions covering orientation, recall of five objects after a delay, money calculation, naming animals in one minute, repeating numbers backwards, clock drawing, shapes, and recall of a short story. Scored out of 30, with cut-offs that depend on education.[5] Health-care and social-service professionals, after watching the official training video. Designed for adults aged 60+.[5] 7–10 minutes[1] Developed with, and mostly studied in, US veterans (VA) populations. In the guideline's comparison it was very sensitive (96%) but less specific (61%), so it gives more false alarms.[1]
MMSE
Mini-Mental State Examination		 A 30-point test of orientation, memory, attention, language and copying a drawing; the best-known and most studied brief test.[1][6] A clinician. 7–10 minutes[1] Less sensitive to MCI: in the MoCA validation study it detected only 18% of people with MCI.[2] Scores are affected by education.[7] It is proprietary and must be purchased for clinical use.[1]
AD8
Eight-item Informant Interview		 8 yes/no questions about change compared with how the person used to be — memory, orientation, judgment and everyday function. 2 or more “yes” answers suggests a possible problem.[8] A family member or friend who knows the person well (an “informant”); it can also be answered by the person themselves. No formal training needed to give it.[8] About 2–3 minutes[1][8] A screening tool only — not enough to diagnose dementia.[8] May miss sudden (acute) or very subtle problems.[1] Less accurate when people rate themselves than when an informant answers.[9] Requires a licence for clinical or research use.[8]
RAVLT
Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test — formal memory testing		 Learning and memory in detail: a 15-word list is read 5 times with recall after each, then a second “interference” list, then recall of the first list immediately and again after 20–30 minutes, plus a recognition test. Shows learning over trials, effects of interference, and how much is kept after a delay.[10][11] A trained examiner, usually as part of a neuropsychological assessment.[11] About 7 minutes of testing plus a 20–30 minute delay (filled with other tests)[10] Results must be compared against norms for age (newer norms also adjust for sex).[10] Like other memory tests, repeat testing can raise scores through practice; how much depends partly on whether an alternate version is used.[12]

How clinicians actually use these tests

🏠

Everyday life matters as much as the score

The Alzheimer's Association guideline recommends that clinicians gather reliable information — ideally including from someone who knows the patient well — about changes in thinking, daily activities, mood and behaviour, and senses and movement, using structured questionnaires where possible.[13]

The difference between MCI and dementia hinges on everyday independence: in MCI, complex tasks such as paying bills or managing medications are still done (perhaps with more effort or reminders); in dementia, the person needs help with them.[13]

🧩

No single number decides

“There is no test value that determines the diagnosis of MCI or dementia — it requires integration of multiple layers and types of information and, importantly, clinical judgment.”[1] Someone who notices real problems at work but scores normally on a brief test may need fuller neuropsychological testing — which also gives a baseline to measure future change against.[1]

📅

Change over time is the key evidence

Evidence of decline over time supports a diagnosis of MCI when it can be measured, and people found to be cognitively normal can be monitored over time.[13] The SLUMS, for example, is designed to help detect changes in thinking over time and can be repeated annually.[5]

🔁

Familiarity can raise repeat scores

Taking the same test again often produces a higher score simply from practice — remembering specific items, learning strategies, or getting used to testing. How big this effect is depends on whether alternate versions are used, the person's age and diagnosis, and the time between tests, so clinicians have to take it into account when judging change.[12] This is one reason not to rehearse the actual test items at home.

📓 Help your clinician see the changes

Because everyday changes matter so much, it helps to bring notes: when you noticed a change, what happened (a missed bill, a forgotten medication, getting lost on a familiar route), plus mood, sleep and any medicine changes.
