📚 Games research: what do memory games really do?

For each kind of game: what it exercises, who was studied, what improved, and whether the gains reached everyday life or lasted. Every claim is linked to its source, and every study has its limits spelled out.

ℹ️ The short answer: practising a game reliably makes you better at that game. Evidence that it improves everyday memory is much thinner, and only a few studies followed people for years.[1] Games are a fun way to stay mentally active, but they are not a treatment.

✏️ Crosswords

The trial: crosswords vs computerized games in MCI (NEJM Evidence, 2022)[2]

  • Who: 107 people with mild cognitive impairment (average age 71), at two US sites.
  • What: people were randomly assigned to web-based crossword puzzles or web-based cognitive games, both provided by Lumos Labs. Each group trained at home for 12 weeks (four 30-minute sessions a week), then did six one-week booster blocks up to week 78.
  • Main result: on the ADAS-Cog memory-and-thinking scale (0–70, higher is worse), scores improved slightly with crosswords and worsened slightly with games. The difference at 78 weeks was 1.44 points.
  • Everyday life: the Functional Activities Questionnaire (everyday activities) got worse more with games than with crosswords. A performance-based test of everyday skills showed no difference.
  • Brain scans: the hippocampus and cortex shrank more in the games group.
⚠️ Limits. There was no group without training, so the trial can't say how crosswords compare with doing nothing. The authors say this needs a future trial. For context, they note that the cholinesterase inhibitor donepezil averages 2.67 points better than placebo on the ADAS-Cog. Other limits:
  • Participants were highly educated on average, so the results may not apply to everyone.
  • The sample was small.
  • Secondary results were not adjusted for multiple comparisons.
  • Different MRI scanners were used.
  • One planned test was dropped midway through.
  • During the pandemic, most participants did their booster sessions at home without clinic visits.
[2]

🗂️ Spaced recall

The pilot: a spaced-retrieval app in early Alzheimer's (JMIR Formative Research, 2024)[3]

Spaced retrieval means testing yourself on something at longer and longer gaps. It has been shown to improve long-term memory in Alzheimer's disease.[3]

  • Who: 61 people: 21 young adults, 20 healthy older adults, and 20 people with MCI due to Alzheimer's disease.
  • What: once a day for 4 weeks, they answered 15 questions in an app to learn new facts and relearn name–face pairs. An algorithm tracked how fast each person forgot and chose when to ask again.
  • What improved: every person with MCI remembered the studied facts better than at the start, and 19 of 20 improved by more than 10 percentage points, similar to the other groups. Name–face memory also improved, but less.
  • Did it last? No memory loss after a break of about 1 week.
  • Usable? 16 of the 20 people with MCI kept to the app's schedule on their own, and 10 wanted to keep using it.
⚠️ Limits.
  • A small pilot with 20 people with MCI, and no randomised comparison group.
  • Follow-up lasted only about a week after the 4 weeks of use.
  • The improvements were for the facts and names people practised; everyday memory wasn't measured.
[3]

🃏 Picture matching (pairs / "Concentration")

What it exercises: remembering where you saw each picture (visual-spatial memory).

What studies show: we did not find a trial testing whether a matching-pairs game improves memory in older adults. In one non-randomised study in two nursing homes (80 people aged 65+), residents chose four group games, matching pairs among them, and played twice a week for 2 months. Happiness and life satisfaction rose and loneliness fell compared with usual activities. Memory was not measured, and the authors say the results need caution and repeating.[4]

The general picture: a major review found brain training improves the trained task but gives little evidence of better everyday thinking.[1] Play Picture Pairs for enjoyment and practice, not as a proven memory treatment.

🎵 Sequence recall (Simon, number spans, N-back)

What it exercises: working memory, meaning holding a short sequence in mind and repeating it.

What studies show: a meta-analysis of working-memory training studies (87 publications, 145 comparisons, people of various ages) found reliable improvements right after training on similar working-memory tasks. It found no convincing improvement in wider abilities such as reasoning, reading or arithmetic. The authors concluded the effects are short-term and specific, and do not generalise to "real-world" thinking skills.[5]

In ACTIVE, older adults' memory-training gains on memory tests had faded by 10 years.[8] Memory training also did not change the rate of diagnosed dementia over 20 years.[7]

🔢 Sudoku and number puzzles

What it exercises: logic, holding options in mind, and attention.

What studies show: in the online PROTECT study, 19,078 healthy volunteers aged 50–93 reported how often they did number puzzles. More frequent puzzlers scored better on all 14 thinking measures, including reasoning, attention, working memory and episodic memory.[6]

⚠️ Limits. This was a one-time snapshot with self-reported puzzle habits, not a trial. It shows a link, but can't show that puzzles cause better thinking: people who already think well may simply enjoy puzzles more. The authors call for more research before treating puzzles as an intervention.[6]

⚡ Visual speed and divided-attention training

ACTIVE: 20-year follow-up of speed training with boosters (2026)[7]

  • What the training was: computer exercises in visual processing and attention, especially divided attention. The tasks got harder as people improved.
  • Who: ACTIVE randomly assigned 2,802 people aged 65 and over, without significant cognitive problems, to speed, memory or reasoning training, or to no contact.
  • Training schedule: 10 initial sessions. People who completed at least 8 were randomly offered booster training at 11 and 35 months (up to four 75-minute sessions each time).
  • Result: using Medicare claims from 1999 to 2019 for 2,021 people, those in speed training who completed at least one booster had a lower risk of a dementia diagnosis (hazard ratio 0.75, 95% CI 0.59–0.95). Speed training without boosters showed no difference (HR 1.01). Memory and reasoning training showed no effect.
  • Everyday life: at 10 years, all trained groups reported less difficulty with daily activities than controls.[8] The 20-year paper also notes earlier ACTIVE findings of fewer at-fault car crashes 6 years after speed training.[7]
⚠️ Limits.
  • Dementia was counted from billing records (a claims-based diagnosis), not from memory testing. People with better healthcare access, watchful family or more education are more likely to be diagnosed.
  • Only people in traditional Medicare were included (about 73% of the sample). Medicare Advantage members, who tend to be healthier, were left out.
  • Boosters were offered only to people who completed most of the first sessions, so selection bias remains possible.
  • ACTIVE has produced many papers, which raises the chance of a finding by luck.
  • One author consults for and owns stock in Posit Science, which markets the speed-training software.
The authors' conclusion is cautious: speed training "has the potential to delay the diagnosis" of dementia.[7]
