# Memory in Old Age: What Changes, Why, How It Is Tested, and What Helps

*An evidence-based write-up for older adults, families and carers. Educational only — not medical advice.*

## 1. The short version

- **Some memory change with age is normal.** The most common changes are slower thinking, needing more time to learn new things, and more "tip-of-the-tongue" moments. These do not usually stop a person living independently.
- **Mild cognitive impairment (MCI)** is a step beyond normal ageing: changes that the person and family notice and that show up on tests, but that do not yet stop everyday activities. Roughly 10–20% of people over 65 have MCI, and the risk rises with age.[[4]](#ref4)
- **Dementia** (of which Alzheimer's disease is the most common cause) is when memory and thinking problems are severe enough to interfere with daily life. About 1 in 9 Americans aged 65+ (an estimated 7.4 million people in 2026) are living with Alzheimer's dementia.[[5]](#ref5)
- **Much risk is modifiable.** The 2024 Lancet Commission estimates that about **45%** of dementia cases worldwide could theoretically be prevented or delayed by tackling 14 risk factors across life.[[1]](#ref1)
- **Memory can be supported at any age** with physical activity, good sleep, treating hearing and vision loss, social and mental engagement, managing blood pressure, diabetes and cholesterol, and by using smart techniques for *building* (encoding) and *finding* (recalling) memories.

## 2. Normal ageing vs. MCI vs. dementia

| | Normal ageing | Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) | Dementia |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical example | Forgetting a name, remembering it later | Often forgetting appointments or recent conversations; others notice | Forgetting recent events entirely; repeating questions; getting lost in familiar places |
| Daily life | Independent | Largely independent, may need more effort or reminders | Needs help with everyday tasks (bills, cooking, medicines, later self-care) |
| On memory tests | Within the expected range for age | Below expected for age and education | Clearly impaired, across several abilities |
| Outlook | Stable, slow change | Some stay stable or improve; roughly 10–15% per year progress to dementia[[3]](#ref3) | Usually progressive; support and treatment can help |

How common is MCI by age? A summary by the Alzheimer's Association (from Petersen et al.) gives roughly **6.7%** at ages 60–64, **8.4%** at 65–69, **10.1%** at 70–74, **14.8%** at 75–79 and **25.2%** at 80–84.[[3]](#ref3) Not everyone with MCI goes on to develop dementia — some return to normal.[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)

### What usually holds up with age
Not everything declines. Ageing can bring some strengths:
- **Vocabulary and general knowledge.** These "crystallised" abilities — knowledge and skills that are overlearned, well-practised and familiar — stay stable or improve gradually through the sixth and seventh decades of life (a person's 50s and 60s).[[19]](#ref19) Many studies show older adults have larger vocabularies and a deeper knowledge of word meanings than younger adults.[[20]](#ref20)
- **Well-practised skills.** Procedural memory — memory for motor and thinking skills, such as how to tie a shoe or ride a bicycle — stays essentially unchanged across the lifespan.[[19]](#ref19)
- **Learning goes on.** Older adults can still learn new skills, form new memories and improve their vocabulary and language skills.[[20]](#ref20)

The changes that *are* common include being slower to find words and recall names, more trouble multitasking, and mild decreases in attention.[[20]](#ref20)

### Everyday example: forgetting medicines
Taking medicines on time is an everyday memory task, so it helps to know what is common and what deserves a check-up.
- **Common, and manageable:** missing a dose now and then. The National Institute on Aging suggests asking your pharmacist in advance, *"If I forget to take my medicine, what should I do?"*, and using meals or bedtime as reminders, or charts, calendars, weekly pill boxes, timers or reminder apps.[[22]](#ref22) The NIA also lists occasionally misplacing things or forgetting to pay a bill as usual signs of mild forgetfulness rather than a serious memory problem.[[21]](#ref21)
- **Worth raising with a doctor:** a *new* difficulty managing your medicines, or increasingly needing memory aids or family members for things you used to handle on your own.[[23]](#ref23) By definition, normal age-related change does not impair daily activities, so new trouble with complex tasks such as managing finances or medications deserves a workup if there is no other obvious explanation (such as a reaction to a medication, a new illness or a vision problem).[[19]](#ref19) In the Alzheimer's Association guideline, people with MCI generally still manage tasks such as paying bills and managing medications, while needing help with them points towards dementia.[[24]](#ref24)
- **Medicines can cause memory problems too:** medication side effects are on the NIA's list of factors that can cause memory problems.[[21]](#ref21) See section 3.4.

The NIA's guide *Memory Problems, Forgetfulness, and Aging* explains what is normal and what is not, and advises people with memory problems to make a follow-up appointment to check their memory every 6 to 12 months: https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/memory-forgetfulness-and-aging-whats-normal-and-whats-not [[21]](#ref21)

### Signs that deserve a doctor's visit
- Asking the same questions repeatedly
- Getting lost in places you know well
- Trouble following recipes, directions or managing bills that used to be easy
- Confusion about time, people or places
- Changes in mood, personality or judgement, or withdrawing from activities
- Family or friends are worried, even if you are not

## 3. Why memory declines: the main causes

### 3.1 Brain changes of normal ageing
- **The hippocampus** — a seahorse-shaped structure central to forming new memories — shrinks about 1–2% a year in older adults without dementia, and that loss is linked to higher risk of cognitive impairment.[[6]](#ref6)
- **The prefrontal cortex** (planning, attention, "working memory") is also affected by ageing, which is why holding several things in mind at once and filtering out distractions get harder.
- **Slower processing speed.** A leading theory (Salthouse) proposes that much of the age difference in memory and reasoning comes from a general slowing of mental operations: if steps take longer, earlier information can fade before later steps are done.[[7]](#ref7) In practice, older adults often remember well when given **more time** and **fewer distractions**.

### 3.2 Diseases that damage the brain
- **Alzheimer's disease** (amyloid plaques and tau tangles), **vascular disease** (strokes, small-vessel damage), **Lewy body disease**, **frontotemporal degeneration**, and mixed causes. Alzheimer's typically begins with trouble learning and retaining new information.

### 3.4 Treatable and reversible causes — rule these out first
The National Institute on Aging and clinical guidance list several conditions that can cause memory problems and often improve with treatment:[[2]](#ref2)[[18]](#ref18)
- **Medication side-effects**, especially *anticholinergic* drugs (some bladder medicines, older antihistamines, some antidepressants and sleep aids), benzodiazepines and opioids. In a large study of adults 65+, the highest cumulative use of strong anticholinergics (over 1,095 standard daily doses across 10 years) was linked with a **54% higher** dementia risk than no use.[[9]](#ref9) *Never stop a medicine without talking to your doctor or pharmacist.*
- **Vitamin B12 (or folate) deficiency**
- **Thyroid problems** (especially an underactive thyroid)
- **Depression, anxiety and stress** — can look like memory loss ("pseudodementia")
- **Poor sleep**, including sleep apnoea. Animal research shows sleep helps the brain clear waste products, including beta-amyloid.[[8]](#ref8)
- **Hearing and vision loss** — if you can't hear or see something clearly, you can't store it well
- **Infections, dehydration, delirium**, heavy alcohol use, head injury, and some tumours or fluid build-up (normal-pressure hydrocephalus)

## 4. How memory is tested

Memory problems are assessed by a clinician using a history (ideally with someone who knows the person well), a physical exam, blood tests (e.g. B12, thyroid), a medication review, and brief cognitive tests — sometimes followed by detailed neuropsychological testing or brain scans.[[18]](#ref18)

### 4.1 Brief screening tests
| Test | What it involves | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Mini-Cog** | Remember 3 words, draw a clock showing "10 past 11", recall the words | Scored 0–5 (1 point per word, 2 points for a normal clock); a score under 3 suggests possible dementia, though 3–5 does not rule out impairment.[[17]](#ref17) Takes ~3 minutes. |
| **MoCA** (Montreal Cognitive Assessment) | 30 points: 5-word delayed recall, trail making, cube copy, clock drawing, naming, attention, fluency, abstraction, orientation | In its validation study, with a cut-off of 26, the MoCA detected 90% of people with MCI, vs. 18% for the MMSE.[[16]](#ref16) |
| **MMSE** (Mini-Mental State Examination) | 30 points: orientation, 3-word recall, attention, language, copying | Long-used; less sensitive to mild problems.[[16]](#ref16) |

### 4.2 Memory and thinking tasks used in research and neuropsychology
- **Word-list learning** (e.g. CERAD 10-word list, Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test): a list is read several times; the person recalls it immediately and again after a delay (often 5–30 minutes). *Delayed recall* is one of the most sensitive early markers of Alzheimer-type change.
- **Story recall** (e.g. Logical Memory): retell a short story immediately and after a delay; scored by the number of "idea units" remembered.
- **Digit span**: repeat a growing series of numbers forwards (attention/short-term memory) and backwards (working memory).
- **Corsi block-tapping**: tap blocks in the same order the examiner did — a visual-spatial version of digit span.
- **Trail Making Test**: connect numbers (Part A) then alternate numbers and letters (Part B) — processing speed and mental flexibility.
- **Clock drawing**: planning, visuospatial skill and executive function.
- **Reaction time / processing speed** tasks: how quickly a person responds; speed slows with age and was a target of the ACTIVE training trial.[[11]](#ref11)
- **Verbal fluency**: name as many animals (or words starting with F) as possible in 60 seconds.

This website includes home-made **practice exercises** (word-list recall, digit span, block tapping, reaction time and story recall). They are not copies of any clinical test, are not validated, and cannot diagnose anything. For a detailed comparison of MoCA, Mini-Cog, SLUMS, MMSE, AD8 and the RAVLT, see the site's "Memory tests doctors use" page (`clinical-tests.html`).

## 5. How to improve memory

### 5.1 Lifestyle evidence
- **Aerobic exercise.** In a randomised trial of 120 older adults, walking 40 minutes, 3 days a week for a year increased hippocampal volume by about 2% (left 2.12%, right 1.97%), while the stretching group lost about 1.4%; the change was linked to better spatial memory.[[6]](#ref6)
- **Multidomain programmes.** The Finnish FINGER trial (1,260 at-risk adults aged 60–77) combined diet, exercise, cognitive training and vascular risk monitoring for 2 years; overall cognitive test scores improved 25% more than in the control group, with larger gains in executive function and processing speed.[[12]](#ref12) The U.S. POINTER trial (2,111 at-risk older adults aged 60–79, 2 years) compared a structured lifestyle programme with a self-guided one. Global cognition (a combined score of executive function, episodic memory and processing speed) improved in both groups, and **overall** it improved faster with the structured programme: by 0.029 standard deviations per year more (95% CI 0.008–0.050).[[15]](#ref15) Looking at the parts separately, executive function improved more with the structured programme, but there was **no difference between the groups in episodic memory** (memory for events and information).[[15]](#ref15) The researchers note that effects on everyday functioning still need study.[[15]](#ref15)
- **Hearing care.** In the ACHIEVE trial, hearing aids plus counselling did not slow cognitive decline in the whole group over 3 years, but in older adults at higher risk, decline was about **48% slower**.[[13]](#ref13)
- **Cognitive training.** In the ACTIVE trial (2,832 adults, mean age 73.6), 10 sessions of reasoning or speed-of-processing training had measurable benefits on those abilities 10 years later, and all trained groups reported less difficulty with daily activities than controls. Memory-training gains on memory tests, however, had faded by 10 years.[[11]](#ref11) *Lesson: practise regularly, and train a variety of skills.*
- **Diet.** Observational studies link Mediterranean/MIND-style eating to better brain health, but a 3-year randomised trial of the MIND diet (604 older adults, both groups with mild calorie restriction) found no significant extra cognitive benefit over the control diet.[[14]](#ref14) A healthy diet is still good for heart and blood vessels, which matter for the brain.
- **Manage the risk factors** listed in section 3.3: blood pressure, LDL cholesterol, diabetes, weight, smoking, alcohol, depression, and get eyes and ears checked.[[1]](#ref1)
- **Sleep** 7–9 hours where possible, and seek help for snoring/apnoea or insomnia.
- **Stay social**: social isolation in later life accounts for an estimated 5% of dementia risk.[[1]](#ref1)

### 5.2 Therapies and treatments
- **Treat reversible causes first.** Memory problems caused by conditions such as depression or blood clots usually go away once the condition is successfully treated.[[21]](#ref21) See section 3.4.
- **Cognitive rehabilitation** is a personalised, goal-focused approach for people with mild-to-moderate dementia. A 2023 Cochrane review (6 trials, 1,702 participants) found high-certainty evidence of large improvements in managing the everyday activities people chose as goals, but negligible effects on memory test scores.[[25]](#ref25)
- **Cognitive stimulation therapy (CST)** offers enjoyable activities for thinking, concentration and memory, usually in small groups. A 2023 Cochrane review (37 trials, 2,766 participants) found a small benefit on cognition (about 2 points on the MMSE), with slight improvements in quality of life and some everyday activities. Few studies checked whether the benefits lasted.[[26]](#ref26)
- **Alzheimer's medicines.** Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine, benzgalantamine) may help reduce or control some symptoms in mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's. Memantine may help some people with moderate-to-severe disease keep certain daily functions a little longer. Lecanemab and donanemab slowed cognitive decline in some people with early Alzheimer's over 18 months, but can cause brain swelling or bleeding (ARIA) and need MRI monitoring. None of these cures Alzheimer's.[[27]](#ref27)
- **Herbal remedies** such as rosemary and gotu kola have not been shown to prevent or treat memory decline; see the site's *Traditional remedies* page (`remedies.html`) for what the studies tested and safety notes.

### 5.3 Building memories (encoding)
1. **Pay attention on purpose.** Say "I'm putting my keys on the hall table" out loud. Most "forgetting" is actually not noticing.
2. **Elaborate.** Connect new information to things you already know (Mr. *Baker* — picture him with flour on his hands).
3. **Chunk.** Group items: 0-7-9-1-2-3-4-5-6 → 079 · 123 · 456.
4. **Dual coding.** Combine words with pictures; draw it, or imagine it vividly.
5. **Method of loci (memory palace).** Place items to remember along a familiar route through your home.
6. **Space it out.** Review after 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 2 weeks, 1 month — spacing beats cramming.
7. **One thing at a time.** Reduce distractions; give yourself more time — processing speed slows with age.[[7]](#ref7)

### 5.4 Recalling memories (retrieval)
1. **Retrieval practice — test yourself.** In a classic study, people who practised recalling a passage remembered 61% after one week vs. 40% for those who only re-read it.[[10]](#ref10)
2. **Use cues.** Go back to where you were when you had the thought; run through the alphabet for a name.
3. **Relax.** Stress blocks retrieval; "tip-of-the-tongue" words often pop up minutes later.
4. **Use external aids without guilt.** Calendars, pill boxes, phone reminders, a "landing spot" for keys and glasses.

## 6. A simple daily brain-healthy routine
- **Morning:** daylight + a 20–40 minute brisk walk; plan the day on a calendar.
- **Mid-morning:** 15 minutes of a challenging activity (a new language, instrument, puzzle, or the games on this site).
- **Lunch:** vegetables, beans, whole grains, fish or nuts; talk with someone.
- **Afternoon:** a social or hobby activity; strength or balance exercise 2–3 days a week.
- **Evening:** recall your day (what you ate, who you spoke to — retrieval practice), review anything you want to learn (spaced repetition), limit alcohol.
- **Night:** consistent bedtime, a dark quiet room, 7–9 hours.
